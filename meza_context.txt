MEZA SCHOLARS - DIGITAL SAT READING AND WRITING ITEM-WRITING RULES
Write original content only. Follow every rule below. The question type you must write is named in the prompt; follow PART 1 and PART 2 for every item, plus the matching section in PART 3.

==================================================
PART 1 - RULES FOR EVERY QUESTION
==================================================

1. FORMAT
- One passage (or a pair of passages labeled "Text 1" and "Text 2") followed by exactly one question with four answer choices and exactly one correct answer.
- The passage (or both texts together) is 25-150 words. Count the words before you finish. Notes in rhetorical synthesis items count toward the limit.
- Target lengths: Standard English Conventions, Transitions, and Words in Context: 25-70 words. Rhetorical Synthesis: 60-120 words of notes. Central ideas and details: 60-110 words. Inferences: 50-120 words. Command of evidence: see its sections (about 50-120 words, growing with difficulty; quotations 20-50). All other types: 80-150 words.
- Use "______" (six underscores) for any blank the student must fill.
- Mark an underlined portion with <u> and </u>.
- Write tables in Markdown, with a title line above the table and units in the column headers.

2. PASSAGE CONTENT
- Subject area: every prompt ends with a REQUIRED SUBJECT AREA. Write the passage on that subject area only, never a different one. The full rotation is life science, history, prose fiction, physical science, social science, earth and space science, humanities, poetry or drama, archaeology and anthropology, and technology and engineering history.
- Within the subject, pick a specific, less common topic. Avoid overused topics: bees and pollination, bean or sunflower seedlings, sea turtles, mangroves, octopuses, crows, coral reefs, and urban heat islands.
- Informational passages read like a condensed academic summary: a researcher, scholar, or group; what they studied or argued; what they found or claimed.
- Literary passages begin with a one-sentence introduction such as "The following text is from a short story." / "...from a poem." / "...from a novel." Add one clause of context if the student needs it (who a character is, the setting). Write the excerpt yourself.
- Self-contained: a student must be able to answer from the passage alone, with no outside knowledge.
- Never give an invented person a first and last name and present them as a real scholar, scientist, or historian. Describe invented experts generically ("one architectural historian," "a team of ecologists"). Full invented names are allowed only for characters in fiction and for authors of invented literary works in quotation items.
- Real-world facts must be widely established and accurate. Never invent a quote from a real person, a real study, or a statistic and present it as fact. When you need a study or data, use a generic invented researcher or group ("a team of ecologists," "students in a biology class") or frame findings as "if true."
- Avoid politically or emotionally charged topics, violence, tragedy, illness, religion, and anything that requires regional or cultural insider knowledge.
- Academic register: third person, formal, no slang, never "you."
- Never mention College Board, never describe the item as official, and never copy, closely paraphrase, or imitate a specific official SAT question.

3. ANSWER CHOICES
- The correct answer must be provable from specific words in the passage or specific data in the table. A careful student must be able to point to the evidence.
- Each wrong choice must be wrong for one clear, identifiable reason. Use these distractor types:
  a. Too broad - claims more than the passage supports.
  b. Too narrow - a true detail that does not answer the question asked.
  c. Reversed - states the opposite of the passage or flips cause and effect.
  d. True but irrelevant - accurate, but does not answer the question.
  e. Word trap - reuses words from the passage while misrepresenting what it says.
  f. Overstatement - absolute language ("proves," "always," "all," "never") the passage does not support.
  g. Misread data - misreports a value or comparison in the table (quantitative items only).
- No two choices may both be defensible. If a wrong choice could be argued for, rewrite it.
- Keep all four choices parallel in length, grammar, and level of detail (how long and complex they are overall depends on difficulty; see section 4). The correct answer must not be the longest or most specific choice by default.
- Never use "all of the above," "none of the above," or "both A and B."
- NO GIVEAWAYS. A test-wise student who skips the passage must not be able to find the key from the choices alone:
  * Length: the key must NOT be the longest choice in most items. In about half your items, make a wrong answer the longest and most detailed choice. Never let the key be the only choice with two clauses or a qualification.
  * Absolutes: at most ONE wrong answer may rely on absolute or extreme wording (only, always, never, all, best, entirely, proves). The others must be wrong for reasons the student can only see in the passage.
  * Tone: if the key is hedged or balanced ("can," "may," "partly"), at least one wrong answer must be hedged or balanced too.
  * Plausibility: even at Easy, every wrong answer should sound reasonable to someone who has not read the passage. Wrong answers are wrong because of the passage, not because they sound silly.
- Every wrong answer should use the passage's topic and vocabulary. A choice about an unrelated topic is too easy to eliminate.

4. DIFFICULTY
If the type name ends in _easy, _medium, or _hard, write the question at exactly that difficulty, and treat the rest of the name as the type (for example, details_easy means an easy details question). Otherwise, divide the Variation number by 3 and use the remainder: 0 = easy, 1 = medium, 2 = hard.
The biggest difference between an easy and a hard question is how many complex ideas the passage juggles at once - not length and not obscure vocabulary alone.
- Easy: a relatively direct text that develops one main idea and does not juggle several complex ideas. Mostly short and medium-length sentences, common vocabulary, and the evidence for the answer is stated plainly in one place. Distractors are clearly off-target on a careful read. Length toward the lower end of the type's range. Answer choices are short and concise: usually one simple idea each, often a brief phrase or a short sentence.
- Medium: a main idea plus one complicating idea (a qualification, an exception, or a contrast between two views). A mix of sentence lengths and some academic vocabulary. One distractor is tempting on a quick read. Answer choices are moderate in length and may combine two related ideas.
- PASSAGE VOCABULARY: medium and hard prompts list words from the Meza vocabulary bank under "VOCABULARY FOR THIS PASSAGE". Sprinkle them in naturally: one advanced word in a medium passage, one or two in a hard passage, never more. The difficulty of a hard passage comes mostly from its ideas and sentence structure; a word or two the student may not know adds to that, but the rest of the passage stays in clear academic English. Exception: central_ideas and details follow their own vocabulary rules; see their shared section.
- Hard: many long sentences that discuss several high-level, abstract ideas at once (for example, a theory, an objection to it, and a qualification, interwoven rather than presented one at a time), with embedded clauses and academic or literary vocabulary. Answering requires tracking how those ideas relate across sentences. Hard passages are slightly longer than easy passages of the same type, but not by much (roughly 10-25% more words, never past the 150-word limit). Two tempting distractors; still exactly one defensible answer. Answer choices are multifaceted and complex: each is typically a full sentence that combines two or more ideas (for example, a claim and its qualification, or a relationship between two parts of the passage), so a distractor can be right about one part and wrong about another.
- Standard English Conventions, Transitions, and Rhetorical Synthesis follow the same principle: difficulty comes from denser sentences and more ideas around the tested point (long intervening phrases, embedded clauses, notes that relate in subtler ways), never from obscure or disputed rules.
- Exception to the answer-length scaling: Words in Context, Transitions, and Standard English Conventions choices stay short at every level (a word, phrase, or punctuation variant). For those, harder means subtler word distinctions and more complex text around the blank.

5. EXPLANATION (required format)
- Start with: "[Domain: X] [Skill: Y] [Difficulty: Z] [Subject: S]", where S is the required subject area name (for example, History) and Domain is one of Craft and Structure, Information and Ideas, Standard English Conventions, or Expression of Ideas; Skill is the exact type name you were given without any _easy/_medium/_hard ending (for example, form_verb_tense); and Difficulty is Easy, Medium, or Hard.
- Then explain why the correct answer is right, citing the specific words or data that prove it.
- Then give one sentence per wrong choice naming why it is wrong.
- The answer choices are shuffled after you write them, so NEVER refer to a choice by its letter. Refer to each choice by quoting its first few words.
- For items built on an invented literary work, end the explanation with "[Invented work]".

==================================================
PART 2 - STANDARD ENGLISH CONVENTIONS: SHARED RULES
==================================================
- Stem for every Standard English Conventions item: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"
- The passage is informational (science, history, the arts) and contains exactly one blank.
- The four choices are the same words differing ONLY in the feature being tested (punctuation, or one verb, pronoun, or noun form). Test one skill per item.
- The text around the blank must make exactly one choice correct in standard written English. Avoid points where style guides disagree (for example, the serial comma).

PART 3 - QUESTION TYPES
==================================================

---------- CRAFT AND STRUCTURE ----------

words_in_context_meaning
- Stem: "As used in the text, what does the word "[word]" most nearly mean?" For a two- or three-word expression, write "phrase" instead of "word" (about one item in five).
- Underline the tested word or phrase in the passage with <u></u>, exactly once. The stem quotes it exactly as underlined.
- The tested word is an everyday word with several common meanings, used in a less common sense. The prompt offers candidate words under "TESTED WORD FOR THIS ITEM". Never use an example word from this rulebook.
- CLUE STRUCTURE: the prompt names a REQUIRED CLUE STRUCTURE. Build the passage around it, as for completion items: unless the structure is "contrast", no contrast words and no "expected, but actually" setup.
- Choices: four short meanings (a word or a phrase of up to four words), the same part of speech as the tested word as used. At least one wrong choice is the word's most common meaning; another may be a second real meaning. Every choice must fit the sentence grammatically, so the student has to decide by meaning, not grammar.
- Do not define the tested word nearby. The sense must come from the logic of the passage, not from a sentence that restates it.
- Difficulty for this type:
  - Easy: a fairly familiar secondary sense; the clue is in the same sentence. No advanced words among the choices.
  - Medium: a less familiar sense; the clue is a sentence away or needs one step of reasoning. One wrong choice is the word's everyday meaning. At most one advanced word among the choices.
  - Hard: an uncommon sense in an abstract, college-level passage. Two wrong choices are real meanings of the word that fit a hurried reading. At most one advanced word among the choices: the difficulty comes from the sense of the tested word, not from obscure choices.

words_in_context_completion
- Stem: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"
- The passage contains one blank. The text must make exactly one choice correct, but the student should have to WORK OUT the meaning from the logic of the passage, not find a synonym sitting next to the blank.
- CLUE STRUCTURE: every words_in_context_completion prompt names a REQUIRED CLUE STRUCTURE. Build the passage around it. Unless the required structure is "restatement", never put a colon, semicolon, or dash right after the blank to explain the word. (On the real test that pattern is uncommon, roughly 1 item in 10-15.)
- DO NOT DEFINE THE ANSWER. No sentence may restate the key's meaning or pile up concrete examples that spell it out (bad: a blank for "exacting" followed by "she fixed every uneven phrase and had players repeat passages until perfect"). The evidence must be indirect: a result, a contrast with what was expected, an evaluation, a consequence someone draws, or a qualification.
- Choices: four words or short phrases of the same part of speech and register. In about one item in four, use short phrases (for example "at odds with", "in keeping with") instead of single words. Distractors fit the grammar but not the logic or precision (wrong direction, wrong degree, wrong connotation). Never offer two near-synonyms that both work.
- Vary the key. Never use a word shown as an example anywhere in this rulebook as the answer.
- Difficulty for this type (overrides the general difficulty rules where they differ):
  - Easy: common academic vocabulary in the passage and the choices. The clue is in the same or the next sentence and fairly direct, but it is still not a definition.
  - Medium: less common words. The clue is at least a sentence away from the blank or needs one step of reasoning (what a contrast or a consequence implies). At least one distractor fits a quick or partial reading.
  - Choice levels: follow the "ANSWER CHOICES FOR THIS ITEM" mix in the prompt. A hard item usually has two words most students don't know, one they half know, and one they know; the correct answer is not always the hardest word. Never make all four choices advanced words.
  - Hard: the passage reads like college-level academic writing about abstract ideas (an interpretation, a theory, the strength of evidence, a scholar's view), with long sentences and advanced vocabulary. The meaning of the blank follows only from how two or more sentences relate, often through a concession or qualification. No concrete detail paraphrases the answer. The key and all three distractors are advanced words with similar connotations, so the student must know precise meanings to choose.

text_structure_function
- Stem: "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?" A sentence is most common; a quotation, phrase, or word may be underlined instead (adjust the stem to match).
- Underline exactly one portion with <u></u>.
- Choices describe roles: introduces a claim, provides an example that supports a claim, presents a counterargument, qualifies an earlier statement, explains a result, offers a hypothesis that is later tested, establishes a contrast. Distractors name a role the portion does not play or misidentify what it relates to.

text_structure_overall
- Stem: "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"
- The passage has a clear two- or three-part movement (claim then evidence; problem then solution; a common view then a challenge to it; a description then a reversal).
- Choices are sequences ("It describes X, then explains Y."). Distractors get the order wrong, include a move the passage does not make, or misdescribe one part.

text_structure_purpose
- Stem: "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"
- Choices begin with infinitives ("To explain...," "To argue...," "To describe...," "To challenge..."). Distractors are too narrow (the purpose of one sentence), the wrong action (argue vs. describe), or overstated.

cross_text_connections
- Two short texts labeled "Text 1" and "Text 2" on the same topic, together 25-150 words. Usually Text 2 responds to, extends, qualifies, or disputes Text 1. Narrative pairs are also allowed.
- Stems (use the one that fits): "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to [the claim/finding] in Text 1?" / "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?" / "How does the author of Text 2 differ from the author of Text 1 in their view of [topic]?" / "In both Text 1 and Text 2, ..." / "What would the author of Text 1 most likely consider [X described in Text 2]?" / "What do these two narrative texts have in common?" / "Which of the following words best describes [subject] as it is portrayed in Text 1 and Text 2?" / "Which of the authors would most likely agree with this statement: '[statement]'?"
- The correct answer requires reading both texts. Distractors represent only one text's view, overstate the disagreement, or attribute a view to the wrong author.

---------- INFORMATION AND IDEAS ----------

central_ideas and details: SHARED RULES (the Central Ideas and Details skill)
(Both types follow these. Where they differ from PART 1, follow these. From a review of 33 real items across all three difficulties.)
- LENGTH: 60-110 words, usually about 85, not counting a literary introduction line. Length stays about the same at every difficulty; difficulty comes from density, not length.
- SENTENCES BY DIFFICULTY: easy: about 4-5 sentences of about 20 words each. Medium: 3-4 sentences of about 24 words. Hard: 2-3 long sentences of about 30-45 words, with dashes, asides, and embedded clauses.
- SUBJECTS: mostly the arts and humanities, science, and literature; some social science and history. The field can be familiar, but the topic is narrow: one study, one person or work, or one scholarly debate. Easy items are often literary excerpts or a simple profile of one person; medium and hard items are almost always informational.
- SHAPE: the prompt names a REQUIRED PASSAGE SHAPE. Build the passage on it.
- TURN WORDS: about two in three passages contain a contrast word: "but" most often, then "although"/"though" and "however"; occasionally "by contrast," "unlike," "rather than," or "until recently." Never use "yet" as a contrast word. Put the turn in sentence 2 or later, often mid-sentence, almost never in sentence 1. The answer usually sits in the turn or right after it. Other signposts lead into conclusions or added evidence ("thus," "in effect," "indeed," "moreover").
- PEOPLE: in science passages, use generic researchers ("a team of ecologists," "researchers"); never invent a named scientist. Arts and history passages may center on one real, well-known person, using only widely established facts. In many informational passages one person's or group's view or finding drives the passage ("one historian argues...," "a team of ecologists found..."), which gives viewpoint questions something to ask about.
- NUMBERS AND DATES: include a number or date in about 4 of 10 informational passages. Only occasionally make the answer depend on comparing them.
- WORDS BY DIFFICULTY: easy: 0-2 harder words; technical terms defined right away. Medium: 1-5 harder words; define subject terms (parentheses, an appositive, a dash, or the next sentence); some academic words may go undefined. Hard: 4-8 harder words; science passages stack subject terms and define only the key one; humanities and social science passages use academic vocabulary without definitions. Words offered under VOCABULARY FOR THIS PASSAGE are optional at easy and medium (at most one); at hard, use one or two.
- PUNCTUATION: dashes, semicolons, and colons belong mostly to medium and hard informational passages. Easy informational passages use none of them.
- LITERARY EXCERPTS: write the excerpt in the style of fiction or poetry published between about 1810 and 1930: period vocabulary and some everyday words in older senses (for example, "owned" meaning "admitted"), even at easy, while the overall gist stays clear. Usually a third-person character, sometimes a first-person narrator. Put the plain introduction on its own line ("The following text is from a novel." / "...from a short story." / "...from a poem."), adding one sentence of context (who a character is, where they are) about half the time. Never invent an author, title, or date. Then a blank line, then the excerpt.
- ANSWER CHOICES:
  - All four choices start with the same subject or frame ("The narrator...," "It...," "They think that...," "Although..."), so only the rest of each choice tells them apart.
  - Usually full sentences. The correct answer grows with difficulty: about 10 words at easy, 14 at medium, 20 at hard. Wrong answers are about as long; the correct answer is not usually the longest.
  - The correct answer restates the idea in more general or synonym terms (a list becomes a category; a mechanism becomes "another explanation was ruled out"). Only easy and medium "According to the text" answers reuse the key passage words. Wrong answers reuse passage words as much as the correct answer does, or more.
  - Hedging (may, likely, some) is uncommon and appears in wrong answers about as often as in correct ones.
  - A comparative or superlative may appear in the correct answer when the passage directly supports it ("older than," "farther than any other"). About one in four wrong answers carries an unsupported one ("more" most often; also "frequently," "less," "earliest," "widely," "most"). Never use "always" or "never" in any choice.
- WRONG ANSWERS: about 6 in 10 are NEVER SAID: a real detail from the passage plus something it does not support (a cause, comparison, preference, reaction, or timeline). Most of the rest are OPPOSITE (the passage's own words with the meaning flipped) or MIX-UP (the wrong person, factor, or time order, or something already known presented as a result). Use HALF RIGHT (one accurate part, one wrong part) mainly on main-idea and relationship items, GOES TOO FAR sparingly, and WRONG TONE only for literary excerpts. Do not write wrong answers that are true but too narrow: every wrong answer must be false or unsupported. In most items, at least one wrong answer reuses a different real detail from the same passage.
- THE TEMPTING WRONG ANSWER BY DIFFICULTY: easy and medium: a WORD ECHO (repeats a striking word or detail from the passage but changes the claim). Hard: a TWIN (the same frame as the correct answer with one clause swapped) or a HALF RIGHT choice; hard wrong answers often differ from the correct one in a single clause. An easy item may include one choice that is easy to rule out; medium and hard items rarely do.
- DIFFICULTY SUMMARY: easy: the answer is stated or one short step away, in one or two sentences; mostly Never said wrong answers and a Word echo lure. Medium: the passage keeps two groups, views, or things apart, and numbers or dates often appear; more Opposite and Mix-up wrong answers. Hard: the answer must be pieced together from two or more sentences over several steps; correct answers are longer and more abstract.

central_ideas
(Main idea items: the question is about the whole passage.)
- STEM: the prompt names a REQUIRED STEM FOR THIS ITEM. Use it word for word. (Most real items use "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"; a few use a question form such as "Which question does the text most directly attempt to answer?" or "Based on the text, which research question was [the researchers'] study most likely intended to answer?", and a few use a small variation.)
- WHERE THE MAIN IDEA SITS: easy informational: stated up front, then examples. Medium: often pieced together from two sentences, most commonly the first and the last. Hard: after the turn in the last sentence, or spread across a finding and its explanation. Literary: the character's overall reaction or situation across the whole excerpt, with no topic sentence. Do not always put the main idea in the last sentence.
- If the passage has a twist, the main idea follows the twist, and the correct answer usually keeps the setup as a concession ("Although X, Y" or "X, but Y"). In hard items, all four choices often start with "Although," so only the second half separates them.
- CORRECT ANSWER: one sentence. At easy, usually one simple idea; at medium and hard, often two joined ideas ("Although X, Y"; "X, but Y"; "X because Y"; "X, which supports Y"). For literary excerpts, say "the narrator," "the speaker," "a character," or "a person," not a character's name.
- WRONG ANSWERS: mostly Never said, then Half right; Wrong tone for literary excerpts. Never a true detail that is merely too narrow.
- QUESTION-FORM STEMS: all four choices are questions ending with "?". The correct one is the question the passage as a whole answers. Wrong ones are questions only one sentence answers, questions the passage raises but does not answer, or questions that use the passage's words but that it never addresses.

details
(Detail items: the question is about one part of the passage, not the whole thing. This is not the "inferences" type, which ends with a blank.)
- STEM: the prompt names a REQUIRED QUESTION FAMILY and FOCUS. Families:
  a. Literal, easy and medium only: "According to the text, ...?" (what is true about [a character]? / what is the subject of [a person's] research? / what is one reason [a group] are interested in [X]? / why are [a group] worried about [X]? / how do [a group] view [X]? / what is a difference between how [a group] view [X] and how they view [Y]? / what does [a character] do as [an action]?)
  b. "Based on the text, ...?", mostly medium and hard: [a person or team] would most likely agree with which statement about [X]? / which choice best describes [X], or the relationship between [X] and [Y]? / what can be concluded about [a trend]? / what would have been the most likely consequence if [X] had not happened? / how do [a group] most likely regard [a character]? / how did [a person] think [a practice] affected [a group]?
  c. Other inference wordings, medium and hard: "What does the text most strongly suggest about [X]?" / "What does the text indicate about [X]?" / "The text makes which point about [X]?" / "It can most reasonably be inferred from the text that [a finding] was important for which reason?" / "Information in the text best supports which statement about [X]?" / "The text most strongly suggests that [X] may have been [done] in part to achieve which effect?"
  Never begin a hard item with "According to the text."
- FOCUS: a trait or description; a person's or group's viewpoint; a reason, cause, or effect; a conclusion from evidence; or another specific detail (a research subject, what a character does, a challenge a group faces, how two things are related).
- CORRECT ANSWER: for a literal item, swap synonyms into the idea of one sentence. For a "Based on the text" or inference item, name the idea at a higher level than the passage does.
- VIEWPOINT ITEMS: wrong answers flip the view, give it to the wrong person or group, or state the very view the person rejects (one item may have two such choices).

command_of_evidence: SHARED RULES (findings, tables, and quotations)
(From a review of 21 real items, 7 at each difficulty. Where these differ from PART 1, follow these.)
- KINDS: findings that would support a claim (about 40%), tables (about a third), and quotations from a literary work (about a quarter, easy and medium only). The claim being tested is almost always the last sentence of the text.
- LENGTH GROWS WITH DIFFICULTY (unlike other Information and Ideas skills): easy texts average about 50 words, medium about 70, hard about 100-120.
- SUBJECTS: science most often, then the arts and music, social science, and literature (quotation items). Topics are narrow: one study, one researcher's idea, one work. Use generic researchers or a student ("a team of ecologists," "a student writing a paper"), never a named invented researcher.
- TURN WORDS: only about a third of texts have one ("although," "while," "but"), mostly medium and hard items that set an earlier view against a new conclusion. Easy items and quotation items basically have none. Never use "yet" as a contrast word.
- WORDS: easy items use plain language. Medium items use some subject terms, and table labels may be technical. Hard items use many technical terms and abbreviations, mostly defined right away in parentheses, with dense definitions; a hard claim may hinge on one key academic word.
- CORRECT ANSWERS do not hedge: findings are written as flat facts (the "if true" in the question does the hedging). The correct answer is the longest choice only about a third of the time.
- WRONG ANSWERS: well over half are TRUE BUT IRRELEVANT: on-topic and accurate-sounding, but they miss the claim's specific point (the wrong aspect, the wrong comparison, or no comparison at all). About a fifth are MISREAD DATA (tables only). A few are OPPOSITE (they would weaken the claim; medium and hard findings), SUPPORTS THE OTHER SIDE or FITS BOTH (only when the text sets up a rival explanation), or HALF RIGHT (one link of a chain, one half of a two-part claim).
- THE TEMPTING WRONG ANSWER: most often a HALF RIGHT choice (part of the claim, such as half a chain, the spread but not the change, or the feeling but not the comparison); otherwise accurate data that doesn't show what's needed, a WORD ECHO (the claim's key word about something else), a finding for the rival explanation, or occasionally a TWIN that flips "different" to "similar." Easy items often have no strong lure.
- Superlatives (highest, largest, lowest) and "significantly" are fine in any choice; they are not a tell here.

command_of_evidence_claim
(Finding items. The prompt names a REQUIRED VARIATION and LOGIC.)
- STEMS: "Which finding, if true, would most directly support the [researchers'] [conclusion/hypothesis/claim]?" (most items) / "Which [detail/statement], if true, would most strongly support the claim in the underlined sentence?" (underline the claim with <u></u>) / "Which quotation from [an expert] would most effectively support the student's [argument/claim]?" / rarely, "Which finding, if true, would most directly undermine the [claim]?"
- SHAPE: background, then a study, then the conclusion in the last sentence. Hard items often put an earlier view or a rival explanation first, then a conclusion that pushes against it. Easy items are a few plain sentences of background, then a student's claim.
- LENGTH: about 95 words (65-115), 3-5 sentences; easy shorter, hard close to 100-120.
- LOGIC BY DIFFICULTY: easy: a straight match; the correct finding basically restates the claim. Medium: test the claim head-on with the claim's own groups and comparison, often after one translation step (a threshold, a code, or a hypothesis that turns measurements into categories); no stated rival explanation. Hard: a two-part claim, a chain (X affects Y, Y affects Z), or a stated rival explanation; the correct finding covers both halves, gives both links, isolates the claimed cause from the rival, or supplies a general fact or mechanism that fills the gap (it may not mention the specific case at all).
- CORRECT ANSWER: about 20-30 words, a flat fact that reuses the claim's key terms and tests exactly what the claim says (same groups, same comparison).

command_of_evidence_quotation
(Quotation items: easy and medium only. The prompt names a REQUIRED GENRE.)
- THE WORK IS INVENTED: give it a made-up title and a made-up author's full name, a year between about 1850 and 1930, and a genre. Make sure the title and the author's name do not belong to a real work or writer. End the explanation with "[Invented work]".
- FORMAT: 20-50 words. Open by naming the work: "[Title] is a [year] [genre] by [author]." Optionally add one sentence of context (who the speaker or narrator is, where they are). Then state the claim and end with a colon and the blank, for example: "In the poem, the speaker conveys a growing impatience with the city: ______"
- STEM: "Which quotation from [title] most effectively illustrates the claim?"
- QUOTATIONS: four invented quotations in the same voice and in the work's period style (old-fashioned diction is fine inside the quotes). Vary their lengths, from a short line to about 40 words; mix poetry with prose (novels, short stories, letters, travel writing, plays). The correct quotation matches the claim's specific point (a time frame, a comparison, a particular target of the feeling) and often says it almost outright, in other words. Wrong quotations are nearly all TRUE BUT IRRELEVANT: a real-sounding quote about the setting, an image, a different time, or a different feeling. Medium items often include two half-claim choices (one shows the feeling but not its target or comparison). Easy items: a one-line claim and quotes that are clearly off-topic except the correct one.

command_of_evidence_quantitative
(Table items. Use tables only, not graphs. The prompt names a REQUIRED TASK.)
- FORMAT: begin the passage field with a titled Markdown table (2-5 rows, 1-3 data columns, units in the title or column headers). Then the text: background and the study, then a claim to support or a sentence ending in "______."
- STEMS: "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the [claim/hypothesis]?" / "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the [statement/example]?" (end the text with "______.").
- LENGTH: from about 25 words (an easy lookup) to about 120.
- TASK BY DIFFICULTY: easy: read one or two values straight off the table; an easy lookup may have one-word or one-number choices. Medium: compare two rows against a claim, usually after a translation step from the text (a threshold or a definition); choices may be long multi-part statements with one wrong detail buried inside. Hard: apply a definition, proposal, or hypothesis from the text to the data, often tracking change over time; sometimes the data run opposite to what a proposal predicts, so the student reasons backward.
- THE TEXT NEVER EXPLAINS ITS OWN LOGIC: do not say which row is the comparison or why a value matters; the student works out which data matter by applying the text.
- CHOICES quote values or describe relative change ("barely changed," "rose sharply," "more than doubled"); never require computing a difference.
- MISREAD DATA: at easy, use a real value from the wrong row or a number that is not in the table, never a comparison flipped in a way checkable in a second. At medium and hard, hide one wrong detail inside an otherwise accurate multi-part statement (a rank off by one, or "significantly" where the gap is small). At medium and hard, most wrong answers are accurate but do not show what the claim needs.

inferences
(From a review of 21 real items, 7 at each difficulty. Where these differ from PART 1, follow these.)
- FORMAT: the passage's last sentence ends with the blank: "______." Stem: "Which choice most logically completes the text?" The blank is always at the very end of the passage.
- PASSAGES: informational only, never a literary excerpt. Arts passages are about an artist or a work, not quoted from one. About a third science; the rest arts and humanities, social science, and history, in roughly that order. Topics are narrow: one study, one scholar's argument, one historical practice. Use generic researchers ("a team of historians," "researchers," "one art historian"), never a named invented person.
- LENGTH: 50-120 words, usually 85-95. Length stays about the same at every difficulty. Easy and medium: 4-6 shorter sentences (about 18 words each), with subject terms defined in parentheses or the next clause. Hard: 2-4 long sentences (about 30 words, some past 40), with dashes, colons, academic vocabulary, and formal logic phrasing (a clause that rules out an alternative, an "only partly explains" qualifier, a conditional). Hard is denser, not longer: keep hard passages around 90-110 words.
- WHAT THE ENDING DOES: the prompt names a REQUIRED ENDING. The completion may say what a finding or set of facts means; explain a puzzle (why two facts don't line up); predict what should happen or what researchers expected; point out what a view or approach leaves out; say what an example shows; or give someone's motive or what a group should take away.
- LEAD-IN AND BLANK: the prompt names a REQUIRED LEAD-IN and BLANK FORM. Lead-ins: a "this finding suggests"-type phrase; a logic word (therefore, thus, consequently, in this way, taken together) with the blank finishing that sentence; or a prediction, an "if this view is right" conditional, or a purpose. Blank forms: a full clause (choices are complete clauses), or the end of the sentence's verb phrase (all four choices start with a verb).
- SHAPE: the prompt names a REQUIRED PASSAGE SHAPE. Build the passage on it.
- TURN WORDS: a bit over half of passages have a turn word ("but" most often, then "however," "although," "while"), usually mid-passage where the finding is set up. Most others still have a built-in contrast (an old belief and new evidence, before and after a change). Never use "yet" as a contrast word. About three in four passages put a logic word in the last sentence.
- STEPS OF REASONING: easy: one step, with the pieces in the last sentence or two (sometimes tied back to a definition or general claim in the first sentence). Medium: one or two steps: combine a finding with the view it pushes against, or put two findings together. Hard: two or three steps, with the pieces spread across the whole passage; a premise in the first sentence usually has to be carried down to the last, and occasionally the student must supply a commonsense assumption the passage never states.
- THE STUDENT BUILDS THE LINK: never lay out every possible outcome and then report which one happened, and never state the key link outright in the passage. In a hard item, state at most one side of a test (for example, only what direct trade would predict), so the student has to infer what the result means. In a medium item, give the evidence indirectly (for example, that most new cardholders had never had a card, rather than that fear of fines had kept them away).
- NUMBERS: numbers and dates are background. The key relationship is stated in words; never make the answer depend on lining up numbers or dates.
- SIDE DETAIL: easy and medium passages include one sentence the answer does not need (background, or an earlier history), and one wrong answer borrows from it.
- CORRECT ANSWER: about 15 words on average: easy and medium 10-15, hard 15-30 (about 22). About half have two parts that mirror two pieces of the passage ("X, provided Y"; "both A and B"; "does X by doing Y"); the rest, mostly easy, are one simple claim. Keep the passage's key nouns but state the new connection in fresh words, never lifting whole phrases. About half of correct answers hedge ("may," "likely," "some," "tend to"); hedge at least one wrong answer in most items too. When the passage sets an old view against a new finding, the correct answer spells out the correction. Never put an absolute or superlative (best, all, only, entirely, every, soon, maximize) in the correct answer. On hard items the correct answer may be the longest choice, because it carries a qualifier or both halves of the idea; give at least one wrong answer a similar two-part length.
- WRONG ANSWERS: more than half are NEVER SAID: a claim on the same topic that the passage doesn't get into (a new comparison, a future outcome, someone's motive). About a fifth are OPPOSITE: either contradicting an earlier sentence, often by restating the old view the passage argues against, or reversing the logic or direction (who did more or less, cause and effect flipped; mostly on hard items). A few GO TOO FAR (stretching a finding into a big claim or prediction). MIX-UP and HALF RIGHT appear mostly on medium and hard items. When the passage overturns an old view, one wrong answer usually restates that old view. Absolutes and superlatives appear only in wrong answers, in about one in six. Comparatives (more, less, fewer) are fine in any choice.
- THE TEMPTING WRONG ANSWER BY DIFFICULTY: easy and medium: a REAL-WORLD TRUE answer, plausible from general knowledge but not supported by the passage (the most common lure). Medium: the old view restated, or an overreach that sounds like the natural takeaway. Hard: a TWIN that keeps the correct answer's structure but flips one direction, or a HALF RIGHT whose first half checks out. An easy item often has no strong lure at all.
- DIFFICULTY SUMMARY: easy: short sentences, one finding, the answer right before the blank, and a correct answer that restates the finding more generally; wrong answers are mostly Never said and sometimes off-topic. Medium: about the same length, but an old view against a new finding or two findings to combine, with the old view and overreaches among the wrong answers. Hard: fewer, longer sentences with formal logic phrasing, pieces spread out (sometimes with an unstated step), longer two-part hedged correct answers, and wrong answers that are direction-flipped twins or half right, so every clause must be checked.

---------- STANDARD ENGLISH CONVENTIONS: BOUNDARIES ----------
(All use the shared stem and rules in PART 2.)

boundaries_end_punctuation
- Tests whether a question or a statement is needed. Direct questions end with "?"; indirect (reported) questions are statements and end with "." (for example, "The researchers wondered whether the birds could see ultraviolet light.").
- Choices differ in word order and/or end punctuation.

boundaries_independent_clauses
- Tests joining two independent clauses. Correct options: a period; a semicolon; a comma plus a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so); a colon when the second clause explains, specifies, or elaborates on the first.
- Distractors: comma splice (comma alone), run-on (no punctuation), conjunction without the comma, colon where the second clause does not explain the first. Only one choice may be correct, so never offer both a correct period and a correct semicolon in the same item.

boundaries_dependent_clauses
- Tests joining an independent clause and a dependent clause (beginning with because, although, while, when, since, if, unless, etc.). Correct: a comma after an introductory dependent clause, or no punctuation when the dependent clause follows the main clause and is essential.
- Distractors: a semicolon or period (creates a fragment), or a colon.

boundaries_transition_punctuation
- Tests punctuation around a conjunctive adverb or transitional phrase (however, therefore, consequently, for example, in contrast) between two independent clauses: semicolon or period before it, comma after it ("...; however, ..."). Within a single clause, the transition is set off by commas.
- Distractors: comma before the transition (comma splice), missing comma after it, punctuation on the wrong side.

boundaries_supplements
- Tests specifying (essential) information vs. additional (nonessential) information. Specifying information is NOT surrounded by commas ("the novelist Toni Morrison," "the students who finished early"). Additional information is set off on both sides by matching punctuation: two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses ("Mars, the fourth planet from the sun, ...").
- Distractors: mismatched pair (comma then dash), one half of the pair missing, commas around specifying information.

boundaries_lists
- Tests punctuating a series: commas between items; a colon introduces a list only after a complete independent clause; semicolons separate items that contain internal commas. Never a colon between a verb or preposition and its list.

boundaries_unnecessary_punctuation
- Tests where punctuation is NOT needed: between subject and verb, between verb and object, between a preposition and its object, before a "that" clause, or between two items joined by "and."
- The correct answer is usually the choice with no punctuation at the blank.

---------- STANDARD ENGLISH CONVENTIONS: FORM, STRUCTURE, AND SENSE ----------
(All use the shared stem and rules in PART 2.)

form_finite_verbs
- Tests the verb form a complete sentence needs. Every sentence needs a main (finite) verb; -ing forms, "to" + verb, and "having" + past participle cannot serve as the main verb.
- Choices: four forms of the same verb (for example, "describes," "describing," "to describe," "having described").

form_subject_verb_agreement
- Tests singular vs. plural verbs. Include one challenge: a phrase between subject and verb ("The collection of letters ______"), an inverted sentence, a compound subject, or a subject such as "each," "every," "a number of," or "the number of."
- Choices: singular and plural forms (for example, "was/were," "has/have," "shows/show") in tenses that let only one fit.

form_verb_tense
- Tests tense and aspect from time clues in the passage: simple past, simple present, present perfect (has/have + past participle: an action that began in the past and continues or still matters; clues like "since," "for decades," "so far"), past perfect (had + past participle: the earlier of two past events; clues like "by the time," "before," "already").
- Choices: four tenses of the same verb; only one matches the time clues.

form_pronouns
- Tests pronoun-antecedent agreement in number (it/its vs. they/their for singular nouns such as "the committee," "the company," "the species") and pronoun case (who/whom, they/them, she/her).
- The antecedent must be unambiguous.

form_possessives
- Tests apostrophes and plural vs. possessive nouns: singular possessive ('s), plural possessive (s'), irregular plurals (children's), its vs. it's, their vs. they're vs. there, whose vs. who's.
- Choices: four spellings of the same word or words (for example, "scientists," "scientist's," "scientists'," "scientist").

form_modifiers
- Tests dangling and misplaced modifiers. The sentence begins with a modifying phrase (for example, "Built in the 1890s, ______"), and the blank begins the main clause. The correct choice places the noun the phrase describes immediately after the comma.
- Choices: four versions of the main clause with different subjects or word orders.

---------- EXPRESSION OF IDEAS ----------

transitions
- Stem: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?"
- The blank is at the start of a sentence (followed by a comma) or after a semicolon. The two ideas have one clear relationship: contrast/concession (however, nevertheless, still), cause/effect (therefore, consequently, as a result), addition (furthermore, moreover, similarly), example (for example, for instance), clarification (in other words, that is), sequence/time (later, subsequently, meanwhile, finally), or emphasis (in fact, indeed).
- Choices: four transitions from DIFFERENT relationship categories, so exactly one fits. Never offer two transitions from the same category.

rhetorical_synthesis
- The passage is: "While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:" followed by 4-6 factual notes, each on its own line starting with "- ". The notes may describe a person, work, study, place, or phenomenon. Use only widely established facts about real subjects, or an invented subject framed hypothetically.
- Stem: "The student wants to [specific goal]. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?" Goals include: emphasize a similarity or difference between two things; introduce [subject] to an audience unfamiliar with it; present a study and its findings; explain a cause or an advantage; make a generalization; emphasize the significance of a detail; specify when or where something happened.
- Choices: four complete sentences, all factually consistent with the notes. Only one accomplishes the exact goal. Distractors accomplish a different goal, cover only part of what the goal requires, or rely on irrelevant notes.
